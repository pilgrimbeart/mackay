# Last Word {#chapter-last-word}

*by David's mother, Valerie MacKay.*

On 6 May 2016, after David's funeral at Barton Woodland Burial site, a lady asked me for a copy of what I had said, so I typed it for her. Last week I found it as I was tidying papers, and perhaps it may be useful now.


\centerline{David MacKay}

Cycling was not a major hobby of David’s. It was, rather, a convenient way of getting from place to place – in Cambridge particularly. But since his newspaper obituaries have conspicuously featured bicycles, you may like to hear the tale of David’s first cycling accident.

One day when he was three years old, his four siblings were keen to make a garden pond. Their father laid out paper computer tape on the grass in a large oval shape. They dug it, lined it with plastic, filled it with water and finished it off with a surround of paving. David decided that for his Mickey Mouse trike this was a purpose built cycle track – no matter that his back inside wheel sometimes hung perilously above the water as he trundled round and round. I told him that if he persisted, he would fall into the water but he trundled on, shoulders hunched, receiver switched off. Rather than pit my will against his, I thought this looked like a potential learning situation - for learning that parents (often) know best.

I ran indoors and set the bath filling with hot water and - sure enough - returned to the garden just in time to see David and trike fall satisfactorily into the pond. Obviously I fished him out too quickly, for when I asked him some 40 years later whether he remembered ever having fallen into the pond, he had no recollection of it. Ach weel, educating the young is not a straight forward business!

Some things, however, he does seem to have absorbed.  I used to tell the children that they were likely to find at school that they were regarded as clever and also as musical but this was not something to be proud of - still less to swank about – as it was not their own doing: it was what God had given them at the start.  What they *did* have control over was how they used their cleverness. Were they clever enough not to quarrel with other children?  Were they they clever enough to work out what would make other children happy to be alive?  Each day is like a sheet of clean paper given to you by God: the question is- what will you write on it?.

Down the years my husband, Donald, built up for the children a collection of maxims. “No singing at the table except for grace” (Grace was said or sung at every meal). But the music and the chatter would keep leaking out. “In a shared acoustic space- one person speaking at a time”! “What are the three magic words that make other people feel warm inside ?” Answer: “Please, Thank you and Sorry”. If they were being unpleasant he would innocently ask: “Now who is trying to make whom happy?”. As they grew older there was added the helpful question: “In this difficult situation what would Jesus do?”. There were other sayings like “Thanked quickly is thanked twice” but the most memorable was still the oldest “What is life for?” and the answer “Making people happy”.

It has given me great pleasure to hear, since David’s death, about the many people to whom David did give life a good flavour. His high school Headmaster wrote to me last week: “What we remember most about David was his kindness to other boys who lacked his exceptional gifts”. From a different quarter I hear that,  beginning when he was still a schoolboy, he spent part of his holidays helping in a Childrens’ Home down in London. The thirty or so children came mostly from unhappy backgrounds and for one reason or another were in care of the local authority. The Home, Mill Grove, has a strongly Christian ethos and is run much more like a family than an institution. David joined in as part of that family, getting alongside the youngsters, joining in their fun, helping with their homework, playing football, and, when he had become a student, organising punting treats here on the Cam. 

It was nearly 30 years ago, in his final year at Cambridge, that David decided that he honestly did not believe in God any more. Up to that time he had been an eager servant and follower of the Lord Christ; busy as a member of his college Christian Union. Yet he did not cease having helpful thought for other folk. He still spent days at the Childrens’ Home and never attempted to shake their trust in God.

When at Cal Tech for his PhD he took pity on some of the undergraduates, who, though very bright, had not been well taught in Mathematics – and started a little Math class for them. His creation of Dasher, his computer app which facilitates writing without a typewriter was triggered in the late 1990s by meeting a severely paralysed patient the rest of whose life was to be spent idle in hospital. In recent months, seeing the fondness you all in the Engineering department have for him and the way he bore his illness, a question has formed in my mind: David dropped God, but did God ever drop David?. 

A picture comes to me of the world – largely scientific – to which David had now limited his mental horizons. The picture is of the conservatory on the South side of our first house - in effect a glass box, cut off from the weather and from the wider spaces of the flourishing orchard beyond. It was home to a beautiful vine whose shapely pale leaves and dark bunches of grapes filled the whole space beneath the glass roof. One day it occurred to me to ask where this vigorous plant found its nourishment and water. I followed its stringy trunk back behind old watering cans and flower pots and found that it came in through a hole in the conservatory wall. The roots were fed from the garden outside!

Valerie MacKay
